Abstract

Compact reranking models span decision-oriented encoders, foundation-based relevance scorers, and candidate-aware architectures. Through an empirical retrospective, this article distinguishes representation architecture, learning objective, and decision readout; examines supervised domain adaptation; and analyzes multi-label ranking on a proprietary evaluation cohort. A matched offline comparison of Qwen ten-feature scoring against archived E5+Laya shows modest point-estimate improvements while leaving statistical significance and serving equivalence unproven. The observed gap between finding an initial relevant item and completely recovering a multi-label set motivates future decision-readout and ranking-objective experiments.

Keywords: neural reranking · compact language models · learning to rank · supervised adaptation · decision readouts · calibration · multi-label ranking

1. Introduction

Modern neural reranking encompasses a much wider design space than the traditional query–document cross-encoder. A model may be built specifically to estimate relevance, adapted from a pretrained generative language model, or designed to examine several candidates jointly. Relevance may be represented by a classifier logit, an affirmative-versus-negative token score, a learned ranking function, or a probability distribution produced by a typed decision head.

These approaches share the objective of ordering candidates, but they do not necessarily learn the same behavior. A pretrained language model may understand a technical requirement while failing to distinguish the most relevant components from superficially related alternatives. Supervised adaptation can improve that distinction, yet optimizing individual relevance labels does not directly guarantee that all relevant candidates will appear within a shallow Top-K.

Consider a requirement with three relevant components: A, B, and C. A reranker that returns A, X, B, Y, C has identified relevant items, but only two of the three are in Top-3. It succeeds at detecting relevance while failing to recover the complete relevance set. The distinction matters when the downstream application cannot process an arbitrarily long shortlist.

This investigation addresses three questions:

  1. Representation: How do specialized encoders, decoder-based language models, and candidate-aware models represent query–candidate relevance differently?
  2. Learning: What ranking behavior do pointwise, pairwise, listwise, and reward-based objectives actually encourage?
  3. Readout: Can a decision head extract relevance more effectively from an existing compact model than the original answer-token scoring interface?

The completed work examines the first two through experiments and architectural review. The third is an explicit research proposal, not a reported experimental result. The study focuses on reranking models; upstream candidate generation and downstream agent execution form evaluation boundaries rather than the central research problem.

2. A Unified View of Reranking Models

A useful comparison of modern rerankers requires separating three design decisions that are often mixed together in model surveys: representation architecture, learning objective, and decision readout. Architecture determines what the model can represent; objective determines which behavior is rewarded during training; readout determines how an encoded representation becomes a relevance judgment.

2.1. Three Orthogonal Design Dimensions

The dimensions are not rigidly coupled. A decoder-only backbone can be trained with pointwise BCE, a pairwise preference objective, or a listwise ranking loss. The same fine-tuned backbone can also support different decision readouts. LoRA is a parameter-adaptation mechanism, not a ranking objective; using Unsloth or LoRA alone does not imply reinforcement learning.

2.2. Specialized Encoder-Based Rerankers

Traditional cross-encoders jointly process a query and document candidate with bidirectional attention and produce a relevance score. An illustrative formulation is:

h = Encoder(query, candidate) and score = wᵀh + b.

BGE-reranker-v2-m3 is a representative specialized relevance model [1]. Its output is a scalar score rather than an autoregressive explanation. Such models can be efficient and straightforward to adapt, though domain-specific relevance still depends on their representations and supervision.

Laya Multilingual represents a different, decision-oriented encoder approach [2]. Its released architecture is based on an mmBERT encoder with typed option scoring and additional decision components. The model family was developed in the context of Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), which targets probabilistic decision behavior. That historical training claim is separate from our local adaptation: we adapted Laya for technical relevance using supervised labels; we did not reproduce the original RLCD procedure. The published checkpoint also notes calibration limitations, so an RLCD origin is not a guarantee of reliable probabilities on a new task.

This makes the comparison with Qwen and Prism more interesting than a comparison of parameter counts. Laya begins with an architecture expressly oriented toward decisions; foundation rerankers reuse broader language-model representations and specialize them toward a constrained relevance task.

2.3. Foundation-Model-Based Rerankers

Qwen3-Reranker extends the Qwen foundation-model family with a relevance-ranking interface [3]. The 0.6B model was the principal compact foundation-based backbone in this study. It can estimate relevance using the difference between affirmative and negative output-token logits:

score(query, candidate) = logit("yes") − logit("no").

The model need not generate a full explanation at inference. It uses the final hidden representation and language-model vocabulary projection to produce a constrained score. This exploits broad pretrained capabilities but does not guarantee that the final score is an optimal decision boundary for a specialized domain.

Prism-Qwen3.5-Reranker-0.8B provides another foundation-model-based approach [4]. Its backbone differs from Qwen3, and the published family supports evidence and contribution-oriented outputs in addition to relevance decisions. The local experiments examined its score-only reranking behavior rather than those broader evidence-generation capabilities.

A key research question was whether fine-tuning the foundation backbone would improve domain-specific discrimination, and whether intermediate domain adaptation added value beyond direct relevance training. A separate question—still untested—is whether the existing yes/no output interface extracts all useful relevance information present in those representations.

2.4. Candidate-Aware Representations

A conventional pairwise-input scorer computes each score independently: sᵢ = f(query, candidateᵢ). Candidate-aware or setwise models can instead condition an item’s estimate on the other candidates in the list: scores = f(query, candidate₁, …, candidateₘ).

Jina-reranker-v3.5 explores listwise candidate interaction using a hybrid-attention architecture [5]. E2Rank integrates candidate-list information into an embedding-oriented ranking formulation [6]. Both are relevant when individually plausible candidates need to be compared in context.

However, listwise inference architecture and listwise optimization are different concepts. Processing several candidates jointly does not prove that a model was trained with a listwise objective, and neither property automatically guarantees better multi-label completeness. Candidate-aware models may also incur additional context, memory, and list-composition costs.

3. What Does a Reranker Actually Learn?

Representation learning and ranking optimization are distinct. The choice of objective determines how positive and negative candidates contribute gradients, which types of mistakes are emphasized, and whether the entire ranked list appears in the training signal.

3.1. A Common Multi-Label Example

For a query with positives {A, B, C} and negatives {X, Y}, consider the result A → X → B → Y → C. At Top-3, Hit = 100%, Recall = 66.7%, and Complete = 0%. The same output can look strong or weak depending on the objective that matters.

This example is not meant to show that any single loss must fail. It illustrates why classification performance, preference accuracy, and full-set recovery should not be treated as interchangeable.

3.2. Pointwise Relevance Learning

Pointwise training predicts whether an individual pair is relevant. Binary cross-entropy penalizes the difference between a binary label and a relevance probability:

BCE = −y log(p) − (1−y) log(1−p).

The training signal is direct, stable, and economical. However, improving confidence in candidate A can reduce BCE even when B or C remains below a shallow cutoff. Grouped BCE collects positive and hard-negative examples around a query, but still optimizes binary relevance; grouping is not equivalent to a listwise ranking loss.

Our Qwen and Prism adaptations used supervised relevance learning of this family. They did not use GRPO, a ranking reward, or RLCD-based policy optimization.

3.3. Pairwise Preference Learning

Pairwise objectives compare a relevant candidate against an irrelevant one. A representative logistic preference loss is:

Pairwise loss = −log σ(score(positive) − score(negative)).

This directly encourages positives to outrank negatives and can penalize X ranking above B or Y ranking above C. The effectiveness depends on pair sampling and weighting, especially when a query has multiple positives and many negatives. Strong average pairwise separation does not itself guarantee that every positive will fit within a fixed Top-K.

3.4. Listwise Ranking Objectives

Listwise methods evaluate a set of candidate scores jointly. Established approaches include ListNet, ListMLE, and differentiable surrogates of ranking metrics [7]. Their benefit is the ability to represent rank interactions and, in some formulations, optimize metrics closer to the actual retrieval objective.

Not every listwise objective supports the same relevance semantics. A softmax objective designed around a single preferred answer does not automatically solve a multi-positive task. To improve complete set recovery, the objective must account for multiple relevant items and their positions relative to the cutoff. Merely changing the label of a training method from pointwise to listwise is insufficient.

3.5. Reinforcement Learning for Reranking

Recent work such as Rank-R1 has explored relevance-label-driven reinforcement learning to improve reasoning-oriented document reranking [8]. These approaches learn from rewards associated with ranking outcomes or reasoning trajectories rather than solely from independent binary labels.

An illustrative ranking reward might combine nDCG@K, Recall@K, and Complete@K with chosen coefficients. This was not the objective used in our experiments. It illustrates how one might explicitly align optimization with shallow multi-label coverage.

Such objectives introduce challenges: Complete@K is discontinuous, can generate sparse reward, and is impossible for queries with more than K gold items. The reward can also privilege an evaluation artifact over a downstream system’s real utility. These costs make a simpler, controlled supervised comparison valuable before undertaking RL-based optimization.

3.6. Probability Calibration Is a Different Objective

A ranking score answers which candidate should come first. A calibrated probability answers how reliable is the numeric confidence. These are not the same property.

For binary logits, a global monotonic transformation such as positive-temperature scaling can change probability calibration while preserving every pairwise ranking order. Hence calibration improvements may improve Brier score, negative log-likelihood, or calibration error without changing Hit@K, Recall@K, or Complete@K.

This separates three lines of work: improved representation learning, improved ordering under a ranking objective, and improved probabilistic decision calibration. Their effects should be measured independently.

4. Jev-Style Decision Readouts and RLCD

4.1. Why Decision Readouts Deserve Separate Attention

Most of our experiments changed the backbone parameters through supervised adaptation. A complementary route is to preserve the backbone and change how a relevance decision is extracted from its hidden representation.

A fine-tuned language model may contain useful domain information in intermediate or final hidden states, even if the original vocabulary projection does not make that information maximally useful for binary relevance. The hypothesis is that a lightweight readout may expose an effective decision boundary without additional backbone fine-tuning.

4.2. Existing Qwen/Prism Decision Mechanism

The current score-only setup reads the difference between final-position yes/no logits, effectively projecting the last hidden state through the existing language-model output head. A different task-specific readout can be fitted as score = wᵀ hₗ + b on a chosen layer’s hidden representation.

Simply requesting the final yes/no logits again is not a new experiment—the rerankers already do that. The meaningful intervention is a new supervised decision head, a calibrated option readout, or a selected intermediate-layer representation. Such a head may be a ridge, LDA, or logistic readout while the backbone remains frozen.

4.3. What AnyJev Actually Contributes

AnyJev investigates Jev-style decision extraction from existing LLMs [9]. Its raw/token-level approach reads restricted answer probabilities and addresses option or label biases. A historical release (v0.2.0) also supported supervised closed-form heads on intermediate hidden states, including ridge and shrinkage-LDA variants, with potential early-exit behavior. In later versions, the project’s APIs changed; the old learned-head interfaces should not be represented as directly available in the newest package.

These techniques test the quality of a readout, not necessarily the model’s underlying knowledge or reasoning. If a frozen hidden-state head works, that would suggest a gap between available representations and the original output interface. If it does not, the current relevance representation or training objective may need to change.

4.4. AnyJev Is Not the Same as RLCD Training

A trained linear head is supervised adaptation on top of frozen features, not reinforcement learning. RLCD is a different learning procedure intended to optimize calibrated probabilistic decisions. OpenJev-RLCD explores RLCD-style optimization for reasoning models [10]. Those methods cannot be inferred from the fact that Unsloth or LoRA was used in training.

The present Qwen/Prism experiments used Unsloth + PEFT/LoRA for supervised relevance fine-tuning, not GRPO, RLCD, or calibration-oriented reward optimization. Nor did they evaluate a newly fitted AnyJev-style hidden-state head.

4.5. Why This Matters for Multi-Label Reranking

A better hidden-state head might improve positive-negative separation, prompt robustness, or calibrated confidence. But if it scores every candidate independently, it remains a pointwise scorer. It cannot be assumed to understand candidate-set dependencies or automatically solve shallow multi-label completeness. The distinction motivates a controlled readout study before moving to more expensive ranking objectives.

5.2. What Was Actually Trained?

The foundation-model experiments adapted Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B and Prism-Qwen3.5-Reranker-0.8B using parameter-efficient LoRA with Unsloth/PEFT infrastructure. The later recipe used BF16 and LoRA rank 16 with alpha 32 [11].

This is not a knowledge-base insertion step in the literal sense, nor was it RLCD or policy-gradient training. The direct and sequential arms changed backbone adapters, but the final objective remained supervised relevance learning.

5.3. Early Specialized-Model Experiments

The initial Laya-assisted stage demonstrated that a compact decision-oriented encoder could contribute useful relevance discrimination. One historical frozen-K100 configuration on 393 development queries achieved 84.48% Hit@5 and 77.86% Complete@5, under its own system-level protocol.

Those figures are not standalone Laya-only model metrics and cannot be placed beside the later 180-query results as if they were matched observations. The later matched comparison instead replays an archived E5+Laya fused scoring configuration over exactly the same 180-query cohort as the Qwen scoring configuration.

6. Multi-Label Ranking Results

The results are best understood through three complementary metrics. Hit@K asks whether at least one relevant item appears within Top-K. Macro Recall@K measures the mean fraction of relevant items recovered per query. Complete@K asks whether every relevant item is present within Top-K. Micro Recall, by contrast, weights each gold query–candidate pair equally, so it is not interchangeable with a per-query macro average.

For the final 180-query cohort, comparing these metrics with the historical E5+Laya scoring configuration on the same frozen K100 supports a matched offline point-estimate comparison. It does not prove improved production quality or statistical significance.

6.1. What the Matched Comparison Establishes

Across the reported cutoffs, the Qwen ten-feature configuration has higher point estimates than the archived E5+Laya configuration. At Top-3, the differences are +2.22 percentage points Hit, +2.83 points Macro Recall, and +2.22 points Complete. At Top-5, Complete increases by +5.00 points. These are observed offline differences between two entire scoring configurations.

The experiment does not isolate the effect of the neural backbone because each configuration uses a different fusion recipe. It also does not establish serving parity: ranking only the first fifteen candidates rather than reranking the full K100 would change the comparison population.

6.2. Cardinality, Ranking Depth, and R-Precision

The cohort has 128 single-label queries (71.11%), 28 two-label queries, 14 three-label queries, and 10 queries with four or more relevant identities. Consequently, aggregate Hit@K is strongly influenced by single-label cases, where finding one candidate is equivalent to complete recovery.

R-Precision measures Recall at a cutoff equal to the number of relevant candidates for a given query: R-Precision(q) = Recall@N(q). It remained approximately 60–64% for N=1–3, while Complete@N fell from 61.72% for N=1 to 21.43% for N=3. Thus, partially recovering a multi-label set is easier than placing every gold item into its own N slots. This pattern remains even after adjusting cutoff depth for cardinality.

The informal K=N+2 heuristic is not supported as a universal rule. For N=3, Recall@5 is 71.43% and Complete@5 is 42.86%, both below 80%. The relevant research question is how cutoff, objective, and representation jointly affect multi-label completeness.

6.3. Uncertainty and Data Separation

The results above are point estimates from 180 queries in 166 duplicate groups. For scale, independent-query 95% Wilson intervals are 79.68–89.95% for Qwen Hit@3 (154/180) and 63.52–76.73% for Complete@3 (127/180). They are descriptive approximations that ignore duplicate-group dependence. There is no verified paired cluster-bootstrap interval for the E5+Laya versus Qwen ten-feature difference, so these intervals cannot establish superiority between configurations.

In the three-label subgroup, all 42 gold identities were present within K100, but only 25 appeared within Top-3 and just 3/14 queries were complete. This identifies ranking headroom that cannot be attributed to candidate absence for that subgroup.

6.4. Runtime and Serving Cost

The Qwen final study scored 18,000 query–candidate pairs on a consumer NVIDIA GPU. An archived evaluation receipt recorded approximately 1.98 GiB peak CUDA allocation and 3.2 GiB observed GPU memory in that run. These are not portable serving guarantees. End-to-end P50/P95 for the exact ten-feature Qwen comparison, CPU latency, concurrency, and deployment-depth cost remain unmeasured under a matched protocol.

The historical Laya experiments and K100 candidate-depth observations were performed under different serving conditions; they should not be treated as a direct cost comparison. Production replacement would require matched candidate policies, GPU/CPU profiles, and latency budgets.

7. A Missing Experiment: Jev-Style Readouts for Fine-Tuned Rerankers

7.1. Research Hypothesis

The completed work explored different backbone representations and supervised training strategies, but did not isolate the final answer-token readout. The next experiment should ask whether a task-specific head can extract relevance information more effectively from existing frozen Qwen/Prism hidden states.

7.2. Proposed Readout Comparison

Four approaches should be measured on identical frozen backbones and candidates: (A) existing yes/no token-logit difference; (B) calibrated token logits; (C) task-specific final-hidden-state readout; and (D) selected intermediate-hidden-state readout with a possible early exit. The latter two can use a small regularized linear head as an initial experiment. AnyJev historical methods inform the design but are not assumed to be available as drop-in APIs.

7.3. Evaluation Contract

The new heads should be trained on historical training-fold examples only. Held-out evaluation must be separated from head fitting, temperature selection, layer selection, and any fusion re-training. The 180-query cohort must not be used to fit a new decision head.

Results should include Hit@K, Macro Recall@K, Complete@K, R-Precision, nDCG, and cardinality-stratified performance. Calibration should be checked separately through log loss, Brier score, and reliability curves. Cost must report actual latency/throughput/VRAM; simply reading an intermediate state does not save computation unless subsequent layers are skipped.

7.4. Competing Outcomes

An alternative head might improve ranking while keeping the backbone unchanged. A shallow head might preserve relevance quality while reducing latency. A calibrated readout might improve probability quality without changing ranking order. Equally, the current output-token score may already capture nearly all useful ranking information. Each is a valid finding; no improvement should be presumed.

7.5. Why Not Start with RLCD or GRPO?

Before introducing reward optimization, a frozen-head study isolates a simpler question: whether ranking errors arise because useful information is already encoded but poorly read out. If the readout does not improve ranking, subsequent experiments can examine representation adaptation, hard negatives, pairwise/listwise objectives, or RL-based ranking. This sequence reduces experimental confounding and computational cost.

8. Discussion

8.1. Cross-Model Performance Is Configuration-Dependent

The historical comparisons show that ranking quality depends on cutoff and scoring configuration. The matched final evaluation adds evidence that Qwen ten-feature scoring can outperform the archived E5+Laya configuration on the same offline cohort, but does not establish that one backbone is inherently superior or that production serving would improve.

8.2. Sequential Fine-Tuning Shows Mixed Effects

Intermediate domain adaptation did not consistently outperform direct relevance training. More training stages should therefore be treated as a hypothesis with measurable trade-offs, not as an automatic improvement.

8.3. Pointwise Relevance Is Not Listwise Completeness

The large difference between Hit and Complete for multi-label queries is compatible with independently learned relevance scores, but does not prove grouped BCE is the cause. Candidate descriptions, negative sampling, score separation, and model representations may all contribute. A controlled ranking-objective comparison is needed before attributing the gap to one mechanism.

8.4. Candidate Availability Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

The later candidate pool covers 268/274 gold identities (97.81%), yet Top-3 Complete is 70.56% for Qwen. In the three-label subgroup, all 42 gold identities were in the pool but only three queries were fully recovered at Top-3. The remaining issue for these cases is the ordering of available candidates, not discovery of absent ones. This is an evaluation boundary rather than a claim about the broader retrieval pipeline.

8.5. Decision Readouts Are an Independent Experimental Dimension

Our completed work changed adapters and model families but not the basic output-token readout. A frozen hidden-state head can test whether the current representation already contains better relevance evidence than its original scoring interface exposes. Calibration, ranking, and latency must be assessed separately.

8.6. RL-Based Ranking and RLCD Are Different Research Directions

Reward-based ranking can optimize list-level utility; RLCD concerns calibrated probabilistic decisions. A supervised hidden-state head does neither by itself. Keeping these ideas distinct avoids overstating both the completed experiments and potential next steps.

9. Limitations and Research Directions

The exact dataset is proprietary and cannot be independently reproduced from this article. The final 180 queries were training-disjoint but selection-exposed. Subgroup sample sizes are small, particularly the ten queries with at least four relevant items. The comparison does not have paired cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals or matched CPU/GPU serving-cost benchmarks. There is also no matched standalone Laya-vs-Qwen-vs-Prism table under the final ten-feature configuration; comparisons that use different fusion recipes must be interpreted at the configuration level.

The most informative follow-up studies are (i) a paired cluster-bootstrap for the current matched baseline, (ii) a serving-depth and latency comparison with the actual deployment, (iii) frozen hidden-state decision heads for Qwen and Prism, and (iv) a supervised ranking-objective comparison against grouped BCE. These experiments would address concrete uncertainty without expanding model selection unnecessarily.

10. Conclusion

Compact domain-specific reranking is not determined solely by backbone size. It depends on learned representations, the training objective, the decision readout, the size and distribution of relevant candidate sets, and the depth at which a downstream system can use results.

Historical supervised adaptation materially improved pretrained Qwen scores; additional intermediate domain training had mixed results. The final 180-query experiment shows an offline advantage for a Qwen ten-feature configuration over a historical E5+Laya fusion configuration under a shared candidate pool and evaluator. At Top-3, Qwen achieved 85.56% Hit, 78.33% Macro Recall, and 70.56% Complete; these values should be read together rather than reduced to the strongest headline.

The cardinality analysis demonstrates that detecting one relevant item is different from recovering the full set. A natural next step is to determine whether the current models already encode useful relevance information that can be extracted through a better task-specific decision head—before incurring the complexity of further backbone adaptation or reinforcement-learning-based ranking.

Better domain-specific reranking requires aligning representation, learning objective, decision readout, evaluation metric, and real serving constraints.
