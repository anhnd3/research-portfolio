Abstract

Compact language models provide several approaches to relevance reranking, from specialized bidirectional encoders to foundation-model-based scorers and candidate-aware architectures. Their effectiveness in specialized domains depends not only on model capacity, but also on the relevance representations they learn, the training objectives used to adapt them, and the mechanism by which model outputs become ranking decisions.

This report examines these dimensions through a review of contemporary reranking and decision-model approaches and a series of experiments on a proprietary multi-label relevance-ranking task. The experiments investigate specialized decision encoders, pretrained and fine-tuned foundation-model rerankers, direct task adaptation, sequential domain-to-task training, and a fixed feature-augmented scoring configuration.

Supervised adaptation substantially improved pretrained reranking performance, while intermediate domain training provided no consistent advantage. More importantly, cardinality-aware evaluation revealed that identifying an initial relevant candidate and recovering an entire relevance set are substantially different ranking objectives. At a cutoff matched to the number of relevant items, relevance coverage remained relatively stable across queries with one to three labels, whereas complete recovery declined considerably.

A related system-level finding is that stronger candidate coverage does not guarantee higher recovery at the target Top-K. Owner-related scoping can help, but its benefits depend on how candidates are constructed and promoted; no reliable query-zone gate has yet been established.

These observations motivate a distinction between learning better relevance representations and learning better ranking decisions. In particular, lightweight decision readouts applied to intermediate or final hidden states offer a complementary research direction to further backbone fine-tuning or reinforcement learning. The report concludes by specifying a controlled Jev-style readout experiment for existing fine-tuned rerankers, without conflating this approach with reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions.

1. Introduction

Modern neural reranking encompasses a much wider design space than the traditional query–document cross-encoder.

A model may be built specifically to estimate relevance, adapted from a generative language model, or designed to reason jointly over several candidates. Relevance may be represented by a classifier logit, an affirmative-versus-negative token probability, a learned ranking function, or a distribution produced by a typed decision head.

These approaches share the objective of ordering candidates, but they do not necessarily learn the same behavior.

A pretrained language model may understand a technical requirement without correctly distinguishing the components most relevant to it. Task-specific supervision can improve this discrimination, yet optimizing individual relevance labels does not directly guarantee that all relevant candidates will appear within a shallow Top-K.

This limitation becomes important in multi-label ranking, where a query may have several correct candidates.

Consider three relevant components, A, B, and C. A reranker producing the order A, X, B, Y, C has correctly identified relevant candidates, but only two appear within Top-3. The system succeeds at detecting relevance while failing to recover the complete relevance set.

This raises three questions:

  1. How do specialized encoders and foundation-model rerankers represent relevance differently?
  2. What ranking behavior is encouraged by pointwise, pairwise, listwise, and reinforcement-learning objectives?
  3. Can better decision readouts improve the use of existing learned representations without retraining an entire backbone?

The research retrospective addresses the first two questions through prior experiments and develops the third as a new, explicitly untested research direction.

The report concentrates on reranking models. Candidate generation and downstream agent execution are treated as evaluation boundaries rather than subjects of architectural analysis.

2. A Unified View of Reranking Models

A useful comparison of reranking approaches requires separating three design decisions.

Representation architecture determines how a model encodes queries and candidates.

Learning objective determines what prediction behavior the model is rewarded or penalized for.

Decision readout determines how the learned representation becomes a relevance score, probability, or ranked output.

These three dimensions are related but not interchangeable.

2.1. Three Orthogonal Design Dimensions

Figure 1 · Representation, objective, and decision readout are separate design axes.

The same backbone can support different learning objectives.

The same training objective can be applied to different backbone families.

Likewise, the same pretrained representation can be connected to different decision readouts.

This decomposition is particularly important for interpreting recent LLM-based reranking research, where a change in the scoring head is sometimes confused with a change in the model's underlying reasoning capability.

Models such as BGE-reranker-v2-m3 follow this specialized relevance-scoring paradigm [1].

Their principal advantage is that the architecture and scoring objective are relatively direct.

They do not require natural-language generation to produce a relevance estimate.

However, the pretrained representations may still require adaptation to distinguish concepts in a specialized domain.

Laya as a decision-oriented encoder

Laya Multilingual provides an alternative to the conventional scalar cross-encoder.

Its published architecture combines an mmBERT-based bidirectional backbone with a decision head. The multilingual checkpoint has approximately 322 million parameters, including two additional transformer layers and option-marker scoring components [2].

Laya can produce typed decisions over an explicitly defined answer space.

This differs from a generative model that must first produce language tokens and then interpret them as an answer.

The public model documentation describes its original training as Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD).

However, the released multilingual checkpoint also documents remaining calibration weaknesses.

Therefore, the use of an RLCD training procedure should not be interpreted as proof that the released probabilities are reliably calibrated in every application.

In the present experiments, Laya was further adapted to domain-specific relevance using supervised labels.

The training methodology used for local adaptation was not a reproduction of Laya's original RLCD training.

This separation is necessary when comparing the research history against later Qwen and Prism experiments.

2.3. Foundation-Model-Based Rerankers

Foundation-based rerankers begin with language models pretrained for broad linguistic representation and generation.

Instead, it evaluates a constrained relevance decision using the representations available at its final output position.

The attraction of this approach is transfer learning.

A pretrained language model already contains linguistic and conceptual representations that may be useful for domain-specific ranking.

However, pretrained general knowledge is not equivalent to an appropriate domain-specific decision boundary.

A foundation model may understand both a requirement and a candidate description while assigning a relatively high score to a superficially related but incorrect candidate.

This explains the motivation for supervised relevance adaptation.

Prism and representation differences

Prism-Qwen3.5-Reranker-0.8B uses a more recent foundation-model backbone with a mixture of linear and full attention [4].

Its published model family additionally supports evidence and contribution-oriented outputs.

The local experiments focused on relevance scoring, rather than those broader capabilities.

Consequently, the comparative question was whether different pretrained representations and adaptation strategies improved relevance discrimination, not whether one model had superior general-purpose reasoning ability.

The important distinction is not simply that these models process several items together.

It is that their representation can depend on which other candidates are present.

However, it also introduces list-composition effects, additional memory requirements, and potentially different computational scaling.

Candidate-aware architecture does not automatically imply a listwise training objective, nor does it guarantee improved complete multi-label recovery.

3.2. Pointwise Relevance Learning

Pointwise binary classification trains the model to predict whether each candidate is relevant.

For a query–candidate pair with label :

The model learns individual relevance decisions.

An advantage is that each training example supplies a direct and interpretable supervision signal.

However, the loss does not explicitly optimize the final ordered list.

In the example above, improving the confidence assigned to A may reduce BCE without necessarily moving C above X or Y.

Likewise, a high classification accuracy does not imply that every relevant candidate appears before the Top-K cutoff.

The experiments used grouped BCE, organizing positive and sampled negative candidates by query while retaining a binary classification objective.

Grouping candidates makes training more query-aware, but the mathematical objective remains distinct from a listwise ranking loss.

3.3. Pairwise Preference Learning

Pairwise learning directly compares the relative scores of relevant and irrelevant candidates.

A representative loss is:

The learning signal encourages a positive candidate to outrank a negative candidate.

In the example, it can penalize X outranking B or Y outranking C.

This is more directly aligned with ranking order than independent binary classification.

However, pairwise optimization introduces its own considerations.

A query containing several positives and many negatives yields many possible preference pairs. Their sampling and weighting influence which errors receive the strongest training signal.

Pairwise correctness also does not guarantee a particular Top-K completeness objective.

For example, minimizing average pairwise inversions may still leave one positive candidate below a shallow cutoff.

3.4. Listwise Ranking Objectives

Listwise methods evaluate a collection of candidate scores jointly.

They can directly optimize ranking distributions or differentiable surrogates of ranking metrics.

Examples in the established Learning to Rank literature include ListNet, ListMLE and differentiable ranking-metric objectives [9].

However, listwise learning is a family of objectives rather than one specific algorithm.

Some formulations assume one preferred item or one optimal permutation.

Others support graded relevance or multiple positive candidates.

This distinction matters for domain-specific multi-label ranking.

A listwise objective designed around a single positive answer does not necessarily solve the problem of retrieving all three relevant components within Top-3.

An objective intended to improve multi-label completeness should explicitly consider all relevant candidates and how their positions affect the target metric.

3.5. Reinforcement Learning for Reranking

Recent work has explored using reinforcement learning to improve LLM-based reranking.

Rank-R1 uses reinforcement learning to encourage reasoning before ranking, relying on relevance labels rather than human-annotated reasoning traces [10].

REARANK similarly investigates reasoning-oriented listwise reranking through reinforcement learning [11].

These approaches differ from conventional supervised BCE training in two important ways.

First, the model can learn from a reward associated with its ranking behavior rather than only from individual relevance labels.

Second, a reasoning-oriented model may generate or explore intermediate reasoning trajectories before making a final relevance decision.

The formulation has limitations.

Complete@K is discontinuous and can produce sparse rewards. It is also mathematically impossible when the number of relevant candidates exceeds K.

Furthermore, reward design can introduce incentives that do not align with the real application.

Reinforcement learning is therefore a potentially relevant direction, but not automatically the preferred next step.

3.6. Probability Calibration Is a Different Objective

A ranking score and a calibrated probability should not be treated as equivalent.

Ranking concerns relative order.

Calibration concerns whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed frequencies.

A strictly monotonic transformation, such as positive-temperature scaling of binary logits, preserves the ranking order.

Therefore, temperature calibration can change Brier score, negative log-likelihood or calibration error without changing Hit@K, Recall@K or Complete@K.

This motivates separating two questions:

Can the model distinguish relevant from irrelevant candidates?

Can its numeric scores be interpreted as reliable probabilities?

Both are useful, but improving one does not necessarily improve the other.

4. Jev-Style Decision Readouts and RLCD

4.1. Why Decision Readouts Deserve Separate Attention

Much of the reranking literature concentrates on changing the model backbone or fine-tuning its parameters.

A complementary approach is to preserve an existing model and change how a relevance decision is extracted from its representation.

This distinction is especially relevant to compact foundation models.

Their internal hidden states may already contain information useful for the target task, even when the original output-token scoring mechanism does not expose that information optimally.

The central hypothesis is:

Can a task-specific decision head extract relevance more effectively from an existing representation than the model's original answer-token logits?

This is distinct from asking whether more backbone fine-tuning or more reasoning tokens will improve performance.

4.3. What AnyJev Actually Contributes

AnyJev is an open project exploring Jev-style decisions on existing language models [12].

Its historical approaches include reading restricted answer-token probabilities, reducing option-order bias, applying calibration, and fitting task-specific decision heads.

A particularly relevant method appeared in AnyJev v0.2.0.

That version supported fitting a closed-form ridge or shrinkage-LDA head on intermediate hidden states. The selected layer and head configuration were determined using held-out or cross-validated evidence, without updating the base model's weights.

It also explored early-exit inference by stopping the backbone at the chosen hidden layer.

However, the project subsequently changed direction.

AnyJev v0.3.0 removed the older labeled L1 calibration and L2 closed-form head APIs, focusing its public interface on training-free readouts and self-distilled Tacit models.

Therefore, reproducing the historical hidden-state experiment requires referring to the v0.2.0 implementation or implementing a comparable readout independently. It should not be described as a currently available drop-in API of the latest release.

The original AnyJev head-training level should also not be confused with the Direct versus Sequential Fine-Tuning stages used in this report.

4.4. AnyJev Is Not the Same as RLCD Training

Three related directions should be distinguished.

Direction Changes model weights? Main mechanism Intended outcome
AnyJev raw/L0 readout No Restricted token probabilities and bias correction More consistent typed decisions
Historical AnyJev hidden-state head Backbone frozen Closed-form supervised head Better task-specific decision readout
RLCD-style reinforcement learning Typically yes Reward-based optimization using probabilistic decisions Better calibrated decision behavior

The historical AnyJev readout does not reproduce TypeSafe's unpublished RLCD training.

Likewise, a better supervised head is not evidence that an LLM has learned new reasoning capabilities.

OpenJev-RLCD investigates a different direction, using reasoning traces and proper-scoring-rule feedback to optimize calibrated decisions [13].

Its reported approach is better characterized as reinforcement learning for probabilistic reasoning than as a simple replacement of a relevance-scoring head.

4.5. Why This Matters for Multi-Label Reranking

A decision readout may change the quality of individual relevance estimates.

It may improve separation between positive and negative candidates, reduce sensitivity to prompt formatting, or provide more reliable confidence scores.

However, a binary head that independently scores candidates remains a pointwise decision model.

It does not automatically model relationships among several positive candidates.

Therefore, a Jev-style head could improve ranking quality without necessarily resolving the entire multi-label completeness gap.

This distinction motivates a controlled readout experiment before attempting more expensive reinforcement-learning approaches.

5. Experimental Retrospective

5.1. Task and Evaluation Setting

The empirical task involves ranking technical components against natural-language requirements.

Each query may have one or more relevant candidate identities.

The query text, component identities, descriptions and individual annotations are proprietary and are not reproduced.

The experiments operated on bounded candidate pools supplied by an upstream retrieval stage.

Within a controlled model comparison, candidate membership was held fixed.

The final analysis used 180 queries, 274 known relevant identities and up to 100 candidates per query.

The candidate pool contained 268 of 274 relevant identities, corresponding to 97.81% candidate-level coverage.

Consequently, the measured effectiveness reflects both the candidate pool's limitations and the reranker's ability to order the available candidates.

5.2. What Was Actually Trained?

The experiments used Unsloth, PEFT and LoRA to adapt compact foundation-based rerankers.

Unsloth was the training framework, not the learning objective.

The principal models were Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B and Prism-Qwen3.5-Reranker-0.8B.

Two supervised adaptation strategies were examined.

Direct Task Fine-Tuning: a pretrained reranker was fine-tuned directly on the relevance task.

Sequential Domain-to-Task Fine-Tuning: a pretrained reranker first received supervision derived from technical documentation and was then fine-tuned on the relevance task.

The second stage used grouped BCE with positive and sampled negative candidates.

The later comparative recipe used BF16 and LoRA with rank 16 and alpha 32.

The experiments did not perform RLCD optimization, GRPO, policy-gradient training or an explicit calibration-oriented reinforcement-learning stage.

They also did not fit an AnyJev-style hidden-state decision head.

These distinctions define the boundary between completed work and the next proposed experiment.

5.3. Early Specialized-Model Experiments

The earliest experimental phase investigated Laya as a compact decision-oriented relevance model.

This was followed by experiments with foundation-based rerankers, motivated by the possibility that larger pretrained representations might improve domain-specific discrimination.

These stages established the value of supervised adaptation while also revealing that model selection could depend heavily on evaluation conditions.

6. Multi-Label Ranking Results

6.1. Evaluation Metrics

Let be the known relevant items for query , and the first K ranked items.

Three metrics are central:

Macro Recall averages per-query recall, giving each query equal weight.

These metrics are complementary.

Hit measures whether at least one relevant candidate was found.

Recall measures the fraction of relevance recovered.

Complete measures whether every relevant candidate was recovered.

An additional cardinality-aligned metric is R-Precision:

This evaluates each query at a cutoff equal to its own number of relevant candidates.

It helps separate the effect of a fixed result budget from the quality of the ordering.

6.5. Cardinality-Aligned Evaluation

A fixed cutoff gives queries with different label cardinalities different opportunities for complete recovery.

R-Precision provides an alternative view by evaluating each query at K equal to its relevance count.

R-Precision remained within a relatively narrow range of approximately 60–64% across groups containing one, two or three relevant items.

This is important because the apparent degradation in fixed-cutoff Recall partly reflects the increasing number of relevant items competing for the same positions.

Nevertheless, Complete@N declined considerably as label cardinality increased.

The model often recovered part of the relevant set but did not place every relevant item within the available N positions.

Similar R-Precision therefore does not imply similar multi-label completeness.

6.7. Where Candidate Coverage Stops Becoming Top-K Recovery

The research history exposed a recurring bottleneck that is not visible from the final model leaderboard alone: a relevant candidate can be present in the candidate pool without being promoted into the small Top-K list actually consumed by the application. This is a ranking-order problem, not automatically a retrieval-coverage problem.

A useful decomposition keeps three distinct quantities separate:

  1. Candidate coverage: how many known relevant identities enter the bounded candidate pool?
  2. Gold conversion into Top-K: how many of those relevant identities are promoted into the desired cutoff?
  3. Query completeness: for how many queries are all known relevant identities promoted into Top-K?

The earlier system experiments and later model studies used different cohorts and scoring configurations. The following figures therefore describe separate stages of the research history, not a single matched ablation.

The 454/507 versus 379/507 decomposition is the simplest direct illustration: among 507 gold identities, 379 were surfaced by Top-5, 75 were present but ranked deeper, and 53 did not enter K100. Those categories are mutually exclusive within that particular frozen run. The same arithmetic must not be applied to other cohorts without reconstructing their own candidate sets and rankings.

This early frozen run achieved 352/507 (69.43%) gold identities by Top-3, rising to 379/507 (74.75%) by Top-5 and 408/507 (80.47%) by Top-10. The limited progression helps explain why simply allowing a somewhat deeper shortlist can improve recovery without solving the shallow ranking problem.

The K100→K200 depth sweep was a different frozen scoring configuration. Its +27 additional available gold identities yielded only +5 additional Top-5 hits; it must not be relabeled as a controlled owner-first intervention. The precise consequence is narrower: candidate expansion alone did not reliably convert the newly discovered relevant identities into actionable Top-5 positions.

In the later 180-query study, the same diagnostic emerges at the query level. For the fourteen queries with exactly three gold identities, all 42 gold identities were in the candidate pool, but only 25/42 (59.52%) appeared by Top-3; only 3/14 (21.43%) queries achieved Complete@3. Here the missing Top-3 items were already retrievable, so candidate recall is not the explanation for that subgroup's incompleteness.

The approximately 60–64% R-Precision values are a separate, important observation. At a cutoff matched to the number of known relevant candidates, average partial relevance recovery is similar across these three groups. Yet the probability of recovering every positive falls markedly as cardinality rises. This neither proves a universal model-quality threshold nor shows that multi-label ranking is equally easy; the groups differ substantially in size, and a partial-recovery average is not full-set recovery.

The practical problem is therefore not simply finding more candidates or making K larger. It is improving gold promotion and completeness at an explicitly budgeted Top-K, while respecting the ceiling imposed by available candidates.

6.8. What the Owner-First Experiments Did—and Did Not Establish

Owner-related interventions in the research history operated at different stages of the pipeline. They must not be collapsed into one treatment:

  • Post-fusion owner prior: reorders an unchanged candidate pool using bounded ownership evidence; it does not add a missing gold candidate.
  • Candidate augmentation or scope-first owner selection: changes which candidates enter K100 before truncation and therefore changes the attainable candidate ceiling.
  • Increasing the candidate depth or final output depth: changes a search or consumption budget. It is not itself evidence that ownership information improved ranking.

On the early 393-query / 507-label exploratory system, a bounded post-fusion owner-prior intervention improved Top-5 gold recovery from 372 to 379 identities (+7), without changing the frozen K100 candidate pool. A separate candidate-augmentation arm increased K100 gold coverage from 454 to 467 identities, but none of the thirteen additional in-pool gold identities were promoted into Top-5 in that test. These were useful diagnostics, but not a general owner-first superiority claim.

A subsequent scope-first owner-selection policy on the larger development study produced more substantial gains than those small post-fusion adjustments. Against the corresponding global K100 plus soft-owner-prior comparator, the retained policy was reported to improve Top-5 gold recovery by 74 identities and query-level Complete@5 by 61 queries. This result is about a different candidate universe, not the causal effect of changing the backbone or decision head. The full per-query audit and exact query-zone breakdown are not contained in this public-facing retrospective.

An important negative result remains: neither enlarging the owner-oriented candidate or output window nor stacking several ownership-related signals has yet produced a validated, transferable query zone in which owner-first should be turned on under a simple rule. Candidate factors worth testing include label cardinality, query specificity and length, score margin, ownership confidence, scope ambiguity, and cross-scope dependencies. However, these are candidate gating factors, not an empirically established selector in the present report. It would be incorrect to present them as a discovered policy or assign a fabricated uplift to a supposed optimal zone.

The correct engineering interpretation is mixed. Ownership-aware candidate construction can be valuable where it repairs a weak candidate universe. However, owner-first is not a universal answer to shallow ranking completeness. Additional candidates need to be converted into Top-K positions, and selective owner-first gating requires a properly held-out test that measures both rescued positives and cross-scope losses under a fixed latency and output budget.

The next reproducible analysis would report, per cohort and query stratum, the candidate ceiling, number of gold identities in the pool but below Top-K, owner-first rescues, owner-first losses, and Complete@K. Without the same candidate pool and identical readout/scoring conditions, reranker improvements and scope-policy improvements must be treated as separate claims.

7. A Missing Experiment: Jev-Style Readouts for Fine-Tuned Rerankers

7.1. Research Hypothesis

The completed experiments investigated different pretrained backbones and supervised fine-tuning strategies.

They did not isolate the contribution of the final output-token readout.

The next question is therefore:

Can the relevance information already encoded by a fine-tuned SLM be extracted more effectively through a dedicated decision head?

This can be investigated without immediately changing the backbone or introducing reinforcement learning.

7.2. Proposed Readout Comparison

The proposed experiment would evaluate several scoring mechanisms on a frozen Qwen3 or Prism backbone.

Figure 3. Alternative relevance readouts on a common backbone

Figure 10 · Proposed experiment only: frozen Qwen/Prism backbone with alternative task-specific readouts.

The comparison should include:

A. Existing token-logit baseline. Use the fine-tuned reranker's original affirmative-versus-negative logits. No additional head is fitted.

B. Calibrated token-logit baseline. Fit a temperature on held-out supervision. This tests probability calibration, but should preserve the ordering when the transformation is globally monotonic.

C. Final-hidden-state head. Freeze the backbone and fit a small supervised binary head on the final hidden representation. Compare logistic regression, ridge or an appropriately regularized linear readout.

D. Intermediate-hidden-state head. Extract hidden states at selected depths, fit equivalent heads, and investigate whether useful ranking information can be recovered before the final layer.

The intermediate-head experiment is analogous to the historical AnyJev readout approach, but must be implemented and evaluated independently for the actual reranker architectures.

7.3. What Should Be Measured?

Three groups of outcomes are necessary.

Ranking quality

  • Hit@1/3/5.
  • Macro Recall@3/5.
  • Complete@3/5.
  • R-Precision.
  • Cardinality-stratified performance.

Decision quality

  • Negative log-likelihood.
  • Brier score.
  • Calibration error and reliability curves.
  • Score separation between relevant and irrelevant candidates.

Computational cost

  • Scoring latency under equivalent input and candidate batching.
  • Peak GPU memory.
  • Model blocks executed.
  • Actual inference speedup, if early exit is supported.

The evaluation must use the same query and candidate identities for every arm.

A hidden-state head should be trained only on training-fold examples, with hyperparameter selection and calibration separated from the held-out evaluation data.

The later 180-query batch should not be used to fit or tune the heads.

If the 10-feature fusion is evaluated, its weights and normalization must be trained consistently for each scoring variant using historical OOF predictions. Reusing one model's feature coefficients for another readout would confound the comparison.

Raw model readouts should be evaluated first, before introducing auxiliary signals.

7.4. Expected Outcomes and Interpretation

Several outcomes are possible.

A final-hidden-state head may improve relevance ordering without changing the backbone.

An intermediate-state head may offer comparable ranking quality at lower inference cost.

A calibrated head may improve probability reliability without materially changing ranking metrics.

Alternatively, the existing token-logit readout may already extract nearly all useful information from the fine-tuned representation.

In that case, an additional head would introduce complexity without sufficient benefit.

These alternatives should be treated as competing hypotheses rather than expected improvements.

7.5. Why Not Start with RLCD or GRPO?

Reinforcement learning could eventually optimize a ranking-level reward or calibration objective.

However, it would also change the training methodology and introduce additional sources of experimental complexity.

The lightweight readout experiment isolates a more fundamental question: whether the current representation already contains better relevance information than the original output interface exposes.

If it does, a small head may be sufficient.

If it does not, changing the backbone representation or ranking objective may be necessary.

This makes decision-readout evaluation a useful intermediate research step between supervised LoRA fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning-based ranking.

8. Discussion

8.1. Cross-Model Performance Is Configuration- and Cutoff-Dependent

The progression from E5+Laya to Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B and Prism-Qwen3.5-Reranker-0.8B tested several model families and adaptation recipes, not a controlled scaling law. In the later full-fusion comparison (Section 5.5), Qwen3 Direct led Macro Recall at Top-2, Top-3 and Top-5; Prism Sequential led Top-1, and Qwen3 Sequential led Top-10. The historical E5+Laya baseline remained competitive at Top-3, although it was not a matched model-only experiment.

These observations support a narrower finding: the preferred ranking configuration depended on the evaluation cutoff and scoring setup. Backbone architecture, parameter count, pretraining, fine-tuning objective, negative sampling and auxiliary fusion were not independently held constant. Consequently, the study does not estimate the causal effect of additional pretrained capacity, nor does it establish a universal ordering between Laya, Qwen3 and Prism.

The results also came from iterative model selection, with partially inspected evaluation data and limited paired statistical separation. They should be read as engineering observations in the investigated setting, not as independent replications proving one backbone family superior.

8.2. Sequential Fine-Tuning Shows Mixed, Cutoff-Specific Effects

The standalone development comparison (Section 5.4) modestly favored Sequential Qwen3 on three of four reported metrics, while Direct Qwen3 was slightly stronger on Hit@1. In contrast, the later full-fusion comparison (Section 5.5) favored Direct Qwen3 at Macro Recall@2, @3 and @5, but Sequential Qwen3 at @10. Within Prism, Sequential led at @1, @2 and @3, while Direct led at @5 and @10.

These are different evaluation stages and scoring configurations, so the counts of metric wins must not be pooled into an overall vote. The defensible finding is that an additional domain-training stage did not produce a stable, sufficiently large improvement across the investigated settings. This does not establish that Sequential Fine-Tuning is inferior; its value must be judged under a prespecified target cutoff and comparable evaluation protocol.

8.3. Pointwise Relevance Is Not the Same as Listwise Completeness

The strongest empirical observation concerns the difference between initial relevance detection and complete multi-label ranking.

The model identified at least one relevant item for most queries within Top-3.

However, many additional relevant items remained below the cutoff.

This is compatible with a pointwise training objective that learns useful individual relevance signals without directly optimizing complete set recovery.

Yet the observed result does not establish that pointwise BCE is the sole cause.

Representation quality, negative sampling, score separation, and the candidate distribution remain plausible explanations.

A controlled objective comparison is necessary before attributing the gap to one mechanism.

8.4. Candidate Availability, Gold Promotion and Owner Gating Are Distinct Bottlenecks

The repeated system experiments identify an additional constraint beyond model adaptation. In the early frozen K100 study, 75 of 507 gold identities were already in the candidate pool but were not promoted into Top-5, whereas another 53 were absent from the pool. A separate K100→K200 sweep made 27 more gold identities available but added only five Top-5 hits in its specific scoring setup. In the later three-label subgroup, all gold identities were retrievable but only 25 of 42 reached Top-3.

These observations make conditional in-pool gold conversion as important to evaluate as overall candidate recall. Owner-first candidate scoping can substantially improve the candidate universe in some cohorts, but neither owner priors nor larger cutoffs guarantee that all relevant identities cross the fixed downstream shortlist threshold.

No reliable multi-factor query zone for activating owner-first has yet been established in the evidence reported here. A future owner-gating claim needs matched controls, predeclared features and thresholds, paired rescue/loss accounting and evaluation on queries untouched during tuning. Until then, the unresolved bottleneck is a system-level research finding, not proof that any one backbone, ranking loss or hidden-state head caused it.

8.5. Decision Readouts Are an Independent Experimental Dimension

The research so far emphasized model selection and backbone adaptation.

It did not directly investigate whether the final relevance logits were the optimal readout of the learned representations.

AnyJev-style hidden-state heads offer a way to test this question.

This direction is attractive because it may improve decision quality without another full model adaptation stage.

However, a stronger binary readout would still not automatically provide listwise reasoning or calibrated uncertainty.

These properties must be assessed separately.

8.6. RL-Based Ranking and RLCD Should Not Be Conflated

Reinforcement learning for reranking can use rewards based on candidate ordering, relevance coverage or downstream task success.

RLCD instead emphasizes calibrated probabilistic decisions through reward-based optimization.

These objectives overlap conceptually but are not interchangeable.

Similarly, a closed-form supervised head on a frozen LLM is neither RLCD training nor evidence of additional reasoning.

Distinguishing the methods avoids overstating what has been accomplished and makes the next experiment easier to interpret.

9. Limitations and Research Directions

The empirical results originate from proprietary technical data, limiting independent reproduction of the exact experiments.

The development process was iterative, and the later evaluation cohort was partially inspected during model selection.

The investigated model families also received unequal experimental treatment.

Qwen3 and Prism underwent extensive supervised adaptation. Other architectures primarily informed the broader landscape.

The detailed final analysis covers one 10-feature scoring configuration, not an independently evaluated raw-only deployment.

The data is also dominated by single-label queries. Only ten queries contain four or more relevant items, and no query contains more than seven.

The proposed Jev-style readout experiment has not been performed.

An additional system-level follow-up should audit in-pool gold-to-Top-K conversion, varying the owner-first candidate scope and downstream cutoff independently. A query-zone gate should be reported only if it retains its improvement on a held-out cohort after accounting for cross-scope gold losses and latency. These studies should not be presented as tests of model capacity or decision-head quality.

Future work should prioritize a controlled readout comparison on frozen fine-tuned backbones, followed by cardinality-stratified analysis.

A subsequent experiment could compare pointwise BCE against pairwise or listwise training under identical candidate pools and evaluation conditions.

Reinforcement-learning-based ranking should be considered only when its reward objective, data requirements and computational costs can be justified against simpler supervised alternatives.

10. Conclusion

This retrospective examined compact reranking models through the combined perspectives of representation architecture, training objective and decision readout.

The experiments demonstrated substantial gains from task-specific adaptation, but no consistent advantage from adding an intermediate domain-learning stage.

The detailed multi-label evaluation showed that finding at least one relevant candidate and recovering every relevant candidate within a small Top-K are substantially different objectives.

Across earlier system and later model studies, candidate availability was necessary but insufficient: relevant items already inside a K100 pool were often not promoted into the actionable shortlist, and a separate candidate-depth expansion produced only modest Top-5 gains. Owner-first construction helped in some contexts but no robust, multi-factor query-zone policy was established. These are configuration-specific observations, not a universal verdict on ownership features.

At cardinality-matched cutoffs, R-Precision remained relatively stable across queries with one to three relevant items, while complete recovery declined sharply.

These results motivate more focused investigation into how relevance information is learned and extracted.

The completed experiments used supervised LoRA adaptation with relevance-oriented losses. They did not perform RLCD or reinforcement-learning-based ranking on Qwen3 or Prism.

A natural next step is to test whether lightweight task-specific decision heads can improve the use of representations already learned by these models.

Such an experiment would separate representation learning from decision readout, potentially identifying improvements in ranking discrimination, calibration or inference efficiency without immediately requiring another backbone-training strategy.

The central lesson is that better domain-specific reranking is not only a question of choosing a larger model. It is a question of learning the appropriate relevance representation, optimizing the appropriate objective, and extracting the appropriate decision from that representation.
