Compact Models for Domain-Specific Reranking.

From an E5 + Laya specialized scorer to compact Qwen and Prism rerankers: what improved in complete set recovery, what training explained, and what the experiments cannot isolate.

Central question

Better rankings, but which change mattered?

Three views of one research program: a full-system comparison, a model-only training-objective test, and a separate training-coverage experiment. The same metric has a different meaning when its evaluation contract changes.

01 / The task

When the first answer is not enough

01

In a domain catalog, one request may have several correct answers. A result list can put a correct item first and still leave out something the reviewer needs.

Toy query · three gold items

Relevant set: {A, B, C}. A retrieval system returns A → X → B → Y → C.

01 · A02 · X03 · B04 · Y05 · C
Top-3 2 of 3 relevant found · not completeTop-5 3 of 3 found · complete

Complete@K asks whether every relevant item appears within the first K results; Macro Recall@K gives partial credit. Hit@1 asks only whether the first item is correct. A query with more than K relevant labels cannot achieve Complete@K, even with perfect ordering.

The study evaluates a short-list retrieval task using verified canonical catalog identities. Different aliases for the same item count once. The research question is practical: can smaller trained rerankers recover more complete sets without confusing gains from retrieval, scoring and evaluation?

Readout rule

Compare percentages inside each question, not across the three. The two 594-query analyses have different scoring contracts (full fusion versus model-only). The 387-query evaluation also has a different cohort and gold coverage. Matching a query count does not establish a matched causal comparison.

Across the 594-query evaluations, the candidate pool includes 714 of 753 gold identities (94.82%), with 97 multi-item queries. The 387-query cohort contains 87 multi-item queries and covers 505 of 540 gold identities (93.52%). A reranker cannot rank a missing candidate into the shortlist.

03 / Historical baseline → compact models

What changed from E5 + Laya?

03

The starting point was an E5 + Laya specialized relevance system. A later comparison evaluated Qwen and Prism alternatives. On the shared 594-query candidate pool, the best reported Complete@5 was 82.66% for Qwen Direct, versus 78.11% for E5 + Laya.

Reference · E5 + Laya78.11%Complete@5 · 464/594 queries
Qwen · Direct L282.66%Complete@5 · 491/594 queries
Paired difference+4.55 pp95% CI [+2.53, +6.78] pp

The comparison used 494 duplicate components for a 2,000-draw paired cluster bootstrap. Relative to E5 + Laya, Qwen Direct had 34 Top-5 rescues and seven losses; this was a gain on the observed cohort, not a universal model ranking.

What this does—and does not—show

E5 + Laya uses 12 fused features, including E5; the Prism and Qwen routes use 10 fused features without E5. The result compares complete systems, not a controlled Laya-versus-Qwen backbone substitution.

04 / How models learn

Did the training objective matter?

04

The model-only experiment asks a narrower question: when the scoring interface and evaluation candidates are matched, does grouping a query's positives and negatives improve ranking over individual relevance labels?

Pointwise
relevant / not relevant

Judge each item

Binary cross-entropy rewards the correct label for one query–candidate pair.

Pairwise
A > X

Prefer one over another

A preference loss orders positive versus negative examples. The main Qwen study did not train a matched pairwise arm.

Listwise
softmax(scores)

Compare a training group

Group softmax rewards a designated positive relative to sampled negatives.

Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B still scores one query–candidate pair at inference, using logit(yes) − logit(no). The term listwise here refers to the training objective, not to joint attention over all candidates. [1][2][6][10][12]

Study A · 594 matching queries

Each bar is a query-level completion rate, not a model probability. Values are repeated in Table 1 for non-JavaScript reading.

Both grouped recipes improve Complete@5 over Pointwise BCE by roughly six points: +6.23 pp for Grouped BCE (95% CI +3.99 to +8.62) and +6.40 pp for Listwise + BCE (+3.90 to +9.17). Their head-to-head gap is only 0.17 pp (95% CI −1.72 to +2.00).

On the 97 multi-item queries, Grouped BCE completes 54 by Top-5, Listwise + BCE 52, and Pointwise 50. Strong aggregate numbers therefore do not establish that the listwise term is the best route to recovering all related items.

05 / How much the model sees

Did broader training data matter?

05

The later Qwen experiment held the composite objective fixed and expanded the supervised catalog coverage. It did not hold training exposure or optimizer steps fixed: both changed.

Earlier checkpoint213 / 779 items27.34% positive-item exposure · 758 updates
→
Expanded checkpoint566 / 779 items72.66% positive-item exposure · 2,046 updates

Both models use Listwise cross-entropy + 0.25 × grouped BCE. The common-unseen comparison includes 387 queries and 87 multi-item cases. These results should not be combined with the preceding 594-query figures.

Study B · 387 common-unseen queries

Matched, duplicate-clustered bootstrap comparisons. CIs refer to differences between checkpoints, not each individual accuracy estimate.

Complete@3 improved by +9.04 pp and Complete@5 by +5.94 pp. Among the 87 multi-item queries, the fully recovered counts increased 22 → 32 at Top-3 and 34 → 43 at Top-5. Even after expansion, fewer than half of multi-item queries were complete by Top-5.

Was the gain limited to newly supervised items?

No. Recall@3 improved by +9.67 pp for items newly represented as positives and +8.16 pp for items already represented. These results are compatible with a broader training benefit, but cannot isolate catalog coverage from additional updates or other run differences.

06 / Supporting experiments

Which earlier experiments changed the direction?

06

These runs informed model selection, but they used different development cohorts and input conventions. They are supporting evidence, not rows in a shared leaderboard with the three main comparisons.

Pretrained checkpointsA larger model is not automatically the operational winner.

On a development pool, pretrained Qwen3-4B outscored pretrained Qwen3-0.6B. This is not a paired fine-tuned 0.6B-versus-pretrained 4B cost test.

Document adaptationAdding a preparatory stage had mixed effects.

Qwen's extra adaptation stage showed a small gain; Prism's did not. Different backbones and cohorts prevent a single causal conclusion.

Pairwise pilotAdding a preference term was not consistently beneficial.

The separate Laya pilot used micro recall on one development fold, not the multi-item Complete metric of the main study.

07 / Closing analysis

Key takeaways: what worked, what did not.

07

The study finds measurable gains from task-adapted compact rerankers and stronger supervision. It also shows where complete multi-label recovery remains difficult—and where the experiments cannot isolate the cause of a gain.

+
Supported strengths

What worked

01

The compact full-pipeline configuration improved recovery.

On 594 matched queries, Qwen Direct L2 achieved 82.66% Complete@5, versus 78.11% for E5 + Laya: +4.55 pp (paired 95% CI [+2.53, +6.78] pp).

02

Query-grouped supervision helped in model-only ranking.

Complete@5 increased from 77.10% for pointwise BCE to 83.33% for grouped BCE and 83.50% for Listwise + BCE on the 594-query recipe comparison.

03

The expanded training recipe improved the observed shortlist.

On 387 common-unseen queries, Complete@3 rose 65.89% → 74.94% (+9.04 pp); Complete@5 rose 74.16% → 80.10% (+5.94 pp).

–
Unresolved limitations

What still falls short

01

Multi-item completeness remains the hard case.

In the 594-query full-pipeline study, Qwen Direct L2 completed 52/97 multi-item queries at Top-5 (53.61%). Strong overall results can therefore conceal missed members of a correct answer set.

02

The candidate pool imposes a ceiling.

The frozen K100 pool contained 714/753 gold identities (94.82%). Missing candidates cannot be rescued by any downstream reranker; ranking and retrieval failures need separate diagnoses.

03

The comparisons do not isolate every cause.

E5 + Laya and Qwen/Prism use different fusion features. The expanded run changed positive coverage and update count together. Listwise + BCE was not clearly better than grouped BCE in the matched recipe test.

Practical conclusion

A compact, task-adapted reranker is a credible choice for this evaluated catalog, but a higher overall Top-K score is not the same as complete recovery. The next focused experiment is grouped BCE versus Listwise + BCE on the same expanded data, matched for sampling, seeds and optimization budget. A separate experiment can then test candidate-aware scoring or a frozen-backbone decision head; AnyJev is related future-work context, not an evaluated baseline. [15]

Scope of inference. These are recorded offline experiments, not new training runs or a production-serving benchmark. The comparisons use different evaluation cohorts and candidate/fusion policies where stated; intervals are exploratory and unadjusted for experiment selection. Latency, throughput, and probability calibration were not established. [14][16]
